\section*{TOPIC 1: Budgeting for Procurement Functions}
Budgeting is a foundational component of healthcare procurement, ensuring that all purchasing decisions are guided by planned financial allocations, organizational priorities, and available resources. In health systems where funds are often limited, effective budgeting provides the structure that allows procurement teams to plan, justify, and control expenditures while maintaining transparency and accountability.

\subsection*{1. Procurement Budgeting Concepts}
Procurement budgeting refers to the process of estimating, allocating, and managing financial resources required to support procurement activities within a health facility or organization. It provides a framework for determining what goods and services will be purchased, at what cost, and within which financial period.\\
Key concepts include:

\subsubsection*{a. Planned Expenditure}
Procurement budgets outline expected spending for items such as:

\begin{itemize}
  \item Medicines and medical supplies
  \item Equipment and instruments
  \item Transportation and distribution
  \item Storage, warehousing, and inventory management
  \item Contractual services (e.g., waste disposal, maintenance)
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection*{b. Fiscal Discipline}
A procurement budget promotes financial discipline by setting expenditure limits, preventing overspending, and guiding procurement officers to prioritize essential items.

\subsubsection*{c. Accountability and Transparency}
A clearly defined budget enhances traceability of procurement decisions, supports audit processes, and minimizes risk of misuse of funds.

\subsubsection*{d. Alignment with Organizational Priorities}
The budget ensures procurement supports broader institutional objectives such as quality service delivery, efficiency, and patient safety.

\subsection*{2. Cost Centers in Healthcare Procurement}
A cost center is a unit, department, or activity where costs are accumulated and monitored. In procurement, recognizing cost centers enables accurate allocation and tracking of expenditures. Common procurement-related cost centers include:

\subsubsection*{a. Clinical Departments}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Pharmacy
  \item Laboratory
  \item Radiology
  \item Surgical and maternity units
\end{itemize}

Each department's procurement needs vary depending on services rendered.

\subsubsection*{b. Administrative and Support Services}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Transport and logistics
  \item Maintenance and engineering
  \item Supply chain and stores management
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection*{c. Capital Projects}
Major procurements such as imaging machines, ICT systems, or facility expansion often require separate budget tracking.

\subsubsection*{Why Cost Centers Matter}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Promote accurate cost allocation
  \item Support performance analysis
  \item Enable decisions on cost efficiency and prioritization
  \item Enhance management oversight on spending patterns
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{3. Budget Cycles in Procurement}
The budget cycle refers to the annual sequence of activities involved in planning, approving, executing, and reviewing the budget. Effective procurement depends on understanding this cycle to ensure timely planning and expenditure.\\
A typical procurement-related budget cycle includes:

\subsubsection*{a. Budget Planning (Formulation)}
Procurement teams identify needs, estimate costs, and prepare draft budgets.\\
Tools used:

\begin{itemize}
  \item Annual procurement plans (APP)
  \item Market price surveys
  \item Past expenditure reports
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection*{b. Budget Approval}
The budget is reviewed by management, finance committees, or county/national authorities depending on governance levels. Approval ensures proposed expenditures align with revenue projections.

\subsubsection*{c. Budget Execution}
Funds are released for purchasing activities. Procurement officers follow procedures for requisitions, quotations, tenders, purchases, and payments.

\subsubsection*{d. Monitoring and Control}
Regular tracking of expenditure protects against overspending and detects budget variances early enough for corrective action.

\subsubsection*{e. Evaluation and Reporting}
At the end of the financial year, budget outcomes are reviewed and documented to inform the next planning cycle.

\subsection*{4. Budgeting Methods Used in Procurement}
Different budgeting approaches can be applied depending on institutional priorities, financial capacity, or policy guidelines.

\subsubsection*{a. Incremental Budgeting}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Uses previous year's budget as baseline
  \item Adjustments made for inflation, new needs, or cost changes
\end{itemize}

Advantages: simple, predictable\\
Limitations: may encourage inefficiency by assuming previous costs were justified

\subsubsection*{b. Zero-Based Budgeting (ZBB)}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Every budget item must be justified anew
  \item Assumes no automatic carry-over of past expenditures
\end{itemize}

Advantages: promotes critical review and cost control\\
Limitations: time-consuming and requires strong analytical capacity

\subsubsection*{c. Activity-Based Budgeting (ABB)}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Allocates resources based on activities or processes that drive costs
  \item Useful in supply chain processes where activities such as storage, distribution, and procurement events incur identifiable costs\\
Advantages: accurate and performance-focused\\
Limitations: requires detailed activity analysis
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection*{d. Programme-Based Budgeting (PBB)}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Links procurement costs to specific health programs (e.g., malaria, maternal health)
  \item Facilitates accountability for outcomes and outputs
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection*{Selecting a Budgeting Method}
The chosen method should support transparency, efficiency, and alignment with organizational strategy.

\subsection*{5. Linking Procurement to Financial Planning}
Budgeting is part of a wider financial planning system that ensures resources are available when needed and used efficiently.

\subsubsection*{a. Demand Forecasting}
Procurement teams estimate consumption rates for medicines, supplies, and services, minimizing stock-outs and emergency procurements.

\subsubsection*{b. Cash Flow Planning}
Ensures funds are available throughout the year to support procurement schedules. Poor cash flow planning leads to delayed purchases and service disruptions.

\subsubsection*{c. Alignment with Strategic Plans}
Procurement budgets must support high-level goals such as:

\begin{itemize}
  \item Improving quality of care
  \item Strengthening supply chains
  \item Reducing waste and inefficiencies
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection*{d. Risk Management}
Financial planning includes preparing for price fluctuations, emergencies, and supply disruptions.

\subsubsection*{e. Compliance and Reporting}
Procurement must follow financial controls, audit requirements, and regulatory frameworks outlined in PPADA (2015).

\subsection*{6. Summary of Key Points}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Budgeting is central to responsible procurement management and financial stewardship.
  \item Cost centers help identify where procurement funds are spent and support accountability.
  \item Understanding budget cycles ensures timely planning, expenditure control, and reporting.
  \item Different budgeting methods provide flexibility depending on institutional needs.
  \item Effective budgeting depends on strong financial planning, including forecasting, cash flow management, and alignment with organizational goals.
\end{itemize}
