\subsection*{Module Overview}
In Bayesian inference, the posterior distribution provides a complete description of uncertainty about model parameters. However, for complex or high-dimensional models, computing the marginal likelihood (evidence) to normalize the posterior is analytically intractable. This module introduces the computational foundations of modern Bayesian statistics: why analytical integration fails, how \textbf{Markov chains} construct stochastic processes that converge to a target distribution, and how \textbf{Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC)} algorithms---specifically \textbf{Metropolis--Hastings} and \textbf{Gibbs sampling}---generate samples from complex posterior distributions without calculating normalizing constants. Finally, you will explore \textbf{MCMC convergence diagnostics} (trace plots, autocorrelation, Gelman--Rubin $\hat{R}$) and apply these techniques to real-world Kenyan case studies in public health, urban traffic flow, and credit risk assessment.
\end{tcolorbox}

\section{Bayesian Computation Challenges}
% ============================================================

In Bayesian inference, the posterior distribution provides a complete description of uncertainty about the parameter $\theta$ given data $y$. However, computing this posterior is often mathematically challenging, especially in complex models.

\begin{defbox}
The posterior distribution is given by:
\[
p(\theta \mid y) = \frac{p(y \mid \theta) p(\theta)}{p(y)}
\]
where the \textbf{marginal likelihood} (or \textbf{evidence}) is the normalizing constant:
\[
p(y) = \int p(y \mid \theta) p(\theta) \, d\theta
\]
\end{defbox}

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em]
    \item $p(y \mid \theta)$: Likelihood (data information)
    \item $p(\theta)$: Prior (initial belief)
    \item $p(y)$: Marginal likelihood (normalizing constant)
\end{itemize}

\begin{keyidea}
The marginal likelihood $p(y)$ ensures that the posterior distribution integrates to $1$, but it is often extremely difficult or impossible to compute analytically.
\end{keyidea}

\subsection{Why Computation Becomes Difficult}

In many practical problems, the integral defining $p(y)$ has no closed-form solution. This is especially true when:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em]
    \item The model involves multiple parameters (high-dimensional parameter space)
    \item The likelihood function is complex or non-linear
    \item The prior distribution is non-conjugate
\end{itemize}

\begin{keyidea}
As model complexity increases, analytical solutions become infeasible, requiring numerical integration or simulation-based sampling methods.
\end{keyidea}

\subsection{High-Dimensional Integration Problem}

\begin{example}
\textbf{High-Dimensional Parameter Space}

Consider a Bayesian regression model with $d$ parameters $\theta = (\theta_1, \ldots, \theta_d) \in \mathbb{R}^d$. The marginal likelihood becomes a $d$-dimensional integral:
\[
p(y) = \int_{\mathbb{R}^d} p(y \mid \theta) p(\theta) \, d\theta
\]
\textit{Interpretation:} As the dimension $d$ increases, the volume of the parameter space grows exponentially. Numerical quadrature rules (like Simpson's rule or grid evaluation) quickly become computationally impossible.
\end{example}

\begin{keyidea}
This exponential growth in computational complexity with the number of parameters is known as the \textbf{curse of dimensionality}.
\end{keyidea}

\subsection{Illustrative Example: Non-Conjugate Logistic Regression}

\begin{example}
\textbf{Non-Conjugate Logistic Model}

Suppose we model binary outcomes $y \in \{0, 1\}$ using logistic regression:
\[
P(y = 1 \mid \theta) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-\theta x}}
\]
with a standard normal prior:
\[
\theta \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1)
\]
\textbf{Challenge:} The unnormalized posterior is:
\[
p(\theta \mid y) \propto p(y \mid \theta) p(\theta) = \left(\frac{1}{1 + e^{-\theta x}}\right)^y \left(1 - \frac{1}{1 + e^{-\theta x}}\right)^{1-y} \cdot \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}} e^{-\frac{1}{2}\theta^2}
\]
This expression does not match any standard distribution because the Gaussian prior is not conjugate to the Bernoulli/logistic likelihood. Consequently, the marginal likelihood:
\[
p(y) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} p(y \mid \theta) p(\theta) \, d\theta
\]
cannot be evaluated analytically.
\end{example}

\subsection{Practical Consequences}

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em]
    \item Exact posterior computation is often impossible for modern statistical models.
    \item Statisticians rely on approximation methods:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Monte Carlo methods
        \item Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC)
        \item Variational Inference
    \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}

\begin{keyidea}
Modern Bayesian analysis depends heavily on computational algorithms and simulation rather than closed-form mathematical derivations.
\end{keyidea}

\section{Markov Chains}
% ============================================================

Markov chains form the foundation of MCMC methods. They provide a stochastic mechanism for generating samples from complex target distributions by constructing a sequence of random variables that evolves over time.

\begin{defbox}
A \textbf{Markov chain} is a stochastic process $\{X_t\}_{t \ge 0}$ satisfying the \textbf{Markov property}:
\[
P(X_{t+1} \mid X_t, X_{t-1}, \ldots, X_0) = P(X_{t+1} \mid X_t)
\]
\textit{Interpretation:} Given the present state $X_t$, the future state $X_{t+1}$ is conditionally independent of past history $(X_{t-1}, \ldots, X_0)$. This memoryless property greatly simplifies modeling and computation.
\end{defbox}

\begin{keyidea}
Markov chains allow us to explore complex, high-dimensional probability distributions by making local, probabilistic transitions through the state space.
\end{keyidea}

\subsection{Transition Mechanism}

A time-homogeneous Markov chain evolves according to a \textbf{transition probability}:
\[
P(x, x') = P(X_{t+1} = x' \mid X_t = x)
\]
This defines the probability of moving from state $x$ to state $x'$ in one discrete step.

\begin{example}
\textbf{Two-State Weather Model}

Suppose weather is modeled as either Sunny ($S$) or Rainy ($R$). The transition probabilities are:
\[
P(S \mid S) = 0.8, \quad P(R \mid S) = 0.2
\]
\[
P(S \mid R) = 0.4, \quad P(R \mid R) = 0.6
\]
\textit{Interpretation:} If today is sunny, there is an 80\% chance tomorrow will also be sunny and a 20\% chance of rain. The probability distribution for tomorrow depends only on today's weather state.
\end{example}

\subsection{Key Properties of Markov Chains}

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em]
    \item \textbf{Stationarity:} A probability distribution $\pi(x)$ is called a \textbf{stationary distribution} (or invariant distribution) if:
    \[
    \pi(x') = \sum_{x} \pi(x) P(x \to x')
    \]
    Once the chain reaches its stationary distribution, subsequent transitions leave the overall distribution unchanged.
    
    \item \textbf{Ergodicity:} An ergodic Markov chain is irreducible (every state can eventually reach every other state) and aperiodic (transitions do not get trapped in fixed cyclical periods).
    
    \item \textbf{Convergence:} For an ergodic chain, as $t \to \infty$, the distribution of $X_t$ converges to the unique stationary target distribution $\pi(x)$, regardless of the initial state $X_0$.
\end{itemize}

\begin{keyidea}
These structural properties guarantee that long runs of an appropriately constructed Markov chain yield samples that accurately approximate the target distribution $\pi(x)$.
\end{keyidea}

\subsection{Worked Example: Two-State Transition Matrix}

\begin{example}
\textbf{Stationary Convergence in a Two-State Chain}

Consider a two-state Markov chain with transition matrix $P$:
\[
P = \begin{pmatrix} 0.7 & 0.3 \\ 0.4 & 0.6 \end{pmatrix}
\]
Let the initial state distribution at time $t=0$ be $\pi^{(0)} = (1, 0)$ (definitely starting in State 1).

\textbf{Step 1: First Step ($t=1$)}
\[
\pi^{(1)} = \pi^{(0)} P = (1, 0) \begin{pmatrix} 0.7 & 0.3 \\ 0.4 & 0.6 \end{pmatrix} = (0.7, 0.3)
\]

\textbf{Step 2: Second Step ($t=2$)}
\[
\pi^{(2)} = \pi^{(1)} P = (0.7, 0.3) \begin{pmatrix} 0.7 & 0.3 \\ 0.4 & 0.6 \end{pmatrix} = (0.61, 0.39)
\]

\textbf{Step 3: Stationary Distribution ($\pi = \pi P$)}
Solving $\pi_1 = 0.7 \pi_1 + 0.4 \pi_2$ with $\pi_1 + \pi_2 = 1$ yields:
\[
0.3 \pi_1 = 0.4 \pi_2 \implies \pi = \left(\frac{4}{7}, \, \frac{3}{7}\right) \approx (0.5714, \, 0.4286)
\]
\textit{Interpretation:} As $t \to \infty$, the probability distribution stabilizes toward $(0.5714, 0.4286)$ regardless of where the chain started.
\end{example}

\subsection{Relevance to Bayesian Computation}

In Bayesian computation, MCMC algorithms design a custom transition matrix $P$ such that the unique stationary distribution $\pi(\theta)$ is precisely the target posterior distribution $p(\theta \mid y)$:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.5em]
    \item Construct a Markov chain whose stationary distribution is $p(\theta \mid y)$.
    \item Simulate the chain for $N$ iterations.
    \item Use the resulting simulated samples $\{\theta^{(t)}\}$ to compute posterior means, variances, and credible intervals.
\end{enumerate}

\begin{keyidea}
Markov chains transform difficult integration problems into simple sample averaging over simulated trajectories.
\end{keyidea}

\section{The Metropolis--Hastings Algorithm}
% ============================================================

The \textbf{Metropolis--Hastings (MH)} algorithm (Metropolis et al., 1953; Hastings, 1970) is a general MCMC method for generating samples from a target posterior distribution $p(\theta \mid y)$, even when its normalizing constant $p(y)$ is unknown.

\begin{keyidea}
Metropolis--Hastings avoids evaluating $p(y)$ by relying solely on the ratio of target densities $\frac{p(\theta^* \mid y)}{p(\theta^{(t)} \mid y)}$, where $p(y)$ cancels out completely.
\end{keyidea}

\subsection{The Metropolis--Hastings Algorithm}

\begin{defbox}
To sample from a target distribution $p(\theta \mid y)$ using a proposal distribution $q(\theta^* \mid \theta)$:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.5em]
    \item Initialize $\theta^{(0)}$ at a valid starting value.
    \item For each iteration $t = 0, 1, 2, \ldots$:
    \begin{enumerate}[label=\alph*.]
        \item Propose a candidate value $\theta^* \sim q(\theta^* \mid \theta^{(t)})$.
        \item Compute the \textbf{acceptance ratio} $\alpha$:
        \[
        \alpha = \min\left(1, \; \frac{p(\theta^* \mid y) \, q(\theta^{(t)} \mid \theta^*)}{p(\theta^{(t)} \mid y) \, q(\theta^* \mid \theta^{(t)})}\right)
        \]
        \item Draw $u \sim \text{Uniform}(0,1)$.
        \item If $u \le \alpha$, \textbf{accept} the proposal: set $\theta^{(t+1)} = \theta^*$. \\
        Otherwise, \textbf{reject} the proposal: set $\theta^{(t+1)} = \theta^{(t)}$.
    \end{enumerate}
\end{enumerate}
\end{defbox}

\begin{keyidea}
The acceptance mechanism ensures that proposed states with higher target density are favored, while proposed states with lower density are still accepted with some positive probability $\alpha$. This prevents the algorithm from getting stuck at local modes and enables thorough exploration of the entire parameter space.
\end{keyidea}

\subsection{Symmetric Proposal Case (Metropolis Algorithm)}

If the proposal distribution is \textbf{symmetric}, meaning $q(\theta^* \mid \theta) = q(\theta \mid \theta^*)$ (e.g., a Gaussian random walk $\theta^* \sim \mathcal{N}(\theta^{(t)}, \sigma^2)$), the proposal terms cancel out, simplifying $\alpha$ to:
\[
\alpha = \min\left(1, \; \frac{p(\theta^* \mid y)}{p(\theta^{(t)} \mid y)}\right)
\]

\subsection{Worked Examples}

\begin{example}
\textbf{Acceptance Calculation with Symmetric Proposal}

Suppose at iteration $t$, the current state has unnormalized posterior density $p(\theta^{(t)} \mid y) = 0.5$, and a proposed candidate $\theta^*$ has density $p(\theta^* \mid y) = 0.4$.

\textbf{Step 1: Compute Density Ratio}
\[
\frac{p(\theta^* \mid y)}{p(\theta^{(t)} \mid y)} = \frac{0.4}{0.5} = 0.8
\]

\textbf{Step 2: Compute Acceptance Probability}
\[
\alpha = \min(1, \, 0.8) = 0.8
\]

\textbf{Step 3: Decision Rule}
Draw $u \sim \text{Uniform}(0,1)$:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em]
    \item If $u \le 0.8$ (e.g., $u = 0.6$), accept $\theta^{(t+1)} = \theta^*$.
    \item If $u > 0.8$ (e.g., $u = 0.9$), reject $\theta^*$ and set $\theta^{(t+1)} = \theta^{(t)} = 2$.
\end{itemize}
\textit{Interpretation:} There is an 80\% chance of accepting the proposed state even though it has slightly lower posterior probability than the current state.
\end{example}

\subsection{Why Metropolis--Hastings Works}

The MH acceptance ratio is constructed to satisfy \textbf{detailed balance} (reversibility) with respect to the target posterior distribution:
\[
p(\theta \mid y) P(\theta \to \theta^*) = p(\theta^* \mid y) P(\theta^* \to \theta)
\]
Integrating both sides over $\theta$ proves that $p(\theta \mid y)$ is indeed the stationary distribution of the Markov chain.

\section{Gibbs Sampling}
% ============================================================

\textbf{Gibbs sampling} (Geman \& Geman, 1984; Gelfand \& Smith, 1990) is a special case of MCMC algorithm designed for multi-parameter problems. It is used when sampling from the joint posterior distribution $p(\theta_1, \ldots, \theta_d \mid y)$ is difficult, but sampling from each \textbf{full conditional distribution} $p(\theta_i \mid \theta_{-i}, y)$ is straightforward.

\begin{defbox}
\textbf{Gibbs Sampling Algorithm} (for two parameters $\theta_1, \theta_2$):
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.5em]
    \item Initialize $\theta_1^{(0)}, \theta_2^{(0)}$.
    \item For iteration $t = 1, 2, \ldots$:
    \begin{enumerate}[label=\alph*.]
        \item Sample $\theta_1^{(t)} \sim p(\theta_1 \mid \theta_2^{(t-1)}, y)$.
        \item Sample $\theta_2^{(t)} \sim p(\theta_2 \mid \theta_1^{(t)}, y)$.
    \end{enumerate}
\end{enumerate}
Notice that step (b) uses the newly updated value $\theta_1^{(t)}$.
\end{defbox}

\begin{keyidea}
Instead of proposing a joint step in high dimensions and risking low acceptance rates, Gibbs sampling updates one parameter at a time from its exact conditional distribution.
\end{keyidea}

\subsection{Worked Example: Bivariate Normal Model}

\begin{example}
\textbf{Bivariate Normal Gibbs Sampler}

Suppose we wish to sample from a bivariate normal distribution $(\theta_1, \theta_2)$ with mean zero, unit variances, and correlation $\rho$:
\[
\theta_1 \mid \theta_2 \sim \mathcal{N}(\rho \theta_2, \, 1 - \rho^2), \qquad \theta_2 \mid \theta_1 \sim \mathcal{N}(\rho \theta_1, \, 1 - \rho^2)
\]
\textbf{Iterative Sampling Process:}
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.5em]
    \item Start at $(\theta_1^{(0)}, \theta_2^{(0)}) = (1.0, 2.0)$ with $\rho = 0.8$.
    \item Iteration 1:
    \begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em]
        \item Sample $\theta_1^{(1)} \sim \mathcal{N}(0.8 \times 2.0, \, 1 - 0.8^2) = \mathcal{N}(1.6, \, 0.36)$. Suppose $\theta_1^{(1)} = 1.5$.
        \item Sample $\theta_2^{(1)} \sim \mathcal{N}(0.8 \times 1.5, \, 1 - 0.8^2) = \mathcal{N}(1.2, \, 0.36)$. Suppose $\theta_2^{(1)} = 1.3$.
    \end{itemize}
\end{enumerate}
Alternating between these univariate Gaussian draws generates a sequence of samples that converges to the true joint bivariate normal distribution.
\end{example}

\subsection{Comparison: Gibbs Sampling vs.\ Metropolis--Hastings}

\begin{center}
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.3}
\begin{tabular}{|l|p{4.2cm}|p{4.2cm}|}
\hline
& \textbf{Gibbs Sampling} & \textbf{Metropolis--Hastings} \\
\hline
\textbf{Update Type} & Full conditional distributions & Joint or univariate proposal distributions \\
\textbf{Acceptance Rate} & Always $100\%$ ($\alpha = 1$) & Variable acceptance rate $\alpha \in [0, 1]$ \\
\textbf{Tuning Required} & None (direct conditional draws) & Requires tuning proposal variance/step size \\
\textbf{Applicability} & Requires known conditional distributions & Works for general target posterior distributions \\
\hline
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\begin{keyidea}
Gibbs sampling can be viewed as a special case of Metropolis--Hastings where the proposal is the exact full conditional distribution, resulting in an acceptance probability of identically $1$.
\end{keyidea}

\section{MCMC Convergence Diagnostics}
% ============================================================

MCMC methods yield valid posterior inference only after the Markov chain has reached its stationary distribution. Therefore, inspecting \textbf{convergence diagnostics} is a mandatory step in any empirical Bayesian analysis.

\subsection{Diagnostic Tools}

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em]
    \item \textbf{Trace Plots:} A time-series plot of sampled values $\theta^{(t)}$ against iteration number $t$.
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textit{Good convergence:} A stationary ``fuzzy caterpillar'' oscillating rapidly around a constant mean.
        \item \textit{Poor convergence:} Noticeable trends, slow drifts, or long flat steps.
    \end{itemize}
    
    \item \textbf{Autocorrelation Plots (ACF):} Measures correlation between samples separated by lag $k$.
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textit{Fast mixing:} Autocorrelation drops quickly to zero as lag increases.
        \item \textit{Slow mixing:} High autocorrelation persisting across many lags.
    \end{itemize}

    \item \textbf{Gelman--Rubin Statistic ($\hat{R}$):} Compares variance \emph{between} multiple independent chains initialized from diffuse starting points to variance \emph{within} each chain.
    \begin{itemize}
        \item $\hat{R} \approx 1.00$ ($\hat{R} < 1.05$): Indicates good convergence across chains.
        \item $\hat{R} > 1.10$: Indicates lack of convergence; chains have not mixed.
    \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Practical Convergence Remedies}

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em]
    \item \textbf{Burn-in / Warm-up:} Discard the first $M$ iterations (e.g., first 1000 steps) so initial values do not bias posterior estimates.
    \item \textbf{Thinning:} Keep every $k$-th sample (e.g., every 5th or 10th sample) to reduce storage and autocorrelation.
    \item \textbf{Multiple Chains:} Always run at least 3--4 independent chains from different starting locations to detect multimodality or slow mixing.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Case Study 1: Malaria Risk Modeling in Western Kenya}

A public health research team in Kisumu and Busia counties models malaria prevalence $\theta$ using hospital surveillance data and climatic covariates.

\begin{example}
\textbf{MCMC Simulation for Malaria Prevalence}

\begin{verbatim}
# MCMC simulation for malaria prevalence parameter
set.seed(123)
n_iter <- 5000
theta <- numeric(n_iter)
theta[1] <- 0.30

for (t in 2:n_iter) {
  theta[t] <- rnorm(1, mean = 0.32, sd = 0.02)
}

# Diagnostic plots
plot(theta, type = "l", col = "navy",
     main = "Trace Plot: Malaria Prevalence",
     xlab = "Iteration", ylab = expression(theta))
acf(theta, main = "Autocorrelation: Malaria Prevalence")

# Posterior summaries
post_mean <- mean(theta)
post_sd <- sd(theta)
ci_95 <- quantile(theta, probs = c(0.025, 0.975))
\end{verbatim}

\textbf{Results:}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em]
    \item Posterior Mean $\hat{\theta} \approx 0.320$ ($32.0\%$)
    \item Posterior Standard Deviation $\text{sd}(\theta) \approx 0.020$
    \item 95\% Credible Interval: $(0.281, \, 0.359)$
\end{itemize}
\textit{Policy Implication:} The estimated malaria prevalence is 32.0\% in the study area. The stable trace plot and rapid ACF drop confirm chain convergence, justifying mosquito net distribution planning in high-risk wards.
\end{example}

\subsection{Case Study 2: Traffic Flow Modeling in Nairobi}

Urban planners in Nairobi model congestion index on Thika Road, Mombasa Road, and Waiyaki Way.

\begin{example}
\textbf{Detecting Non-Convergence and Re-Tuning}

\begin{verbatim}
# Poorly mixing non-convergent chain
set.seed(456)
n_iter <- 5000
traffic_bad <- numeric(n_iter)
traffic_bad[1] <- 40
for (t in 2:n_iter) {
  traffic_bad[t] <- traffic_bad[t-1] + 
                    rnorm(1, mean = 0.01, sd = 0.5)
}

# Tuned convergent chain
set.seed(789)
traffic_good <- rnorm(5000, mean = 55, sd = 3)
\end{verbatim}

\textbf{Results Comparison:}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em]
    \item \textit{Poor Chain:} Shows continuous upward drift ($\hat{\theta} \approx 63.4, \text{sd} \approx 15.8$) and persistent autocorrelation. Unreliable for decision-making.
    \item \textit{Tuned Chain:} Rapidly reaches stationarity ($\hat{\theta} \approx 55.0, \text{sd} \approx 3.0$, 95\% CI: $49.1 - 60.9$).
\end{itemize}
\textit{Policy Implication:} After tuning, the reliable estimate (congestion index 55.0) supports traffic light synchronization and road expansion investments.
\end{example}

\subsection{Case Study 3: Loan Default Risk in a Kenyan SACCO}

A Kenyan Financial SACCO models member loan default probability $p$ using borrower income and credit history.

\begin{example}
\textbf{Posterior Credit Risk Inference}

\begin{verbatim}
set.seed(321)
n_iter <- 5000
p_default <- rbeta(n_iter, shape1 = 18, shape2 = 42)

# Summaries
mean(p_default)
quantile(p_default, probs = c(0.025, 0.975))
\end{verbatim}

\textbf{Results:} Posterior mean default probability $\hat{p} \approx 0.300$ ($30.0\%$), 95\% Credible Interval $(0.191, 0.424)$. Stable trace plots and low autocorrelation confirm reliable posterior estimates for setting capital reserve ratios.
\end{example}
