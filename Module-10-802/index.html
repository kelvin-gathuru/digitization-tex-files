MODULE 10
Looking into the Future While Rooted in the Present
Mathematics Instruction  | 6 Instructional Hours

1 MODULE OVERVIEW

This capstone module brings the full arc of the course into focus by examining the emerging technologies that will shape mathematics education over the coming decade, and by grounding that examination in the ethical, equity, and sustainability principles developed throughout previous modules. Artificial Intelligence, Augmented Reality, Virtual Reality, and a cluster of adjacent emerging technologies are no longer speculative — they are already entering classrooms, albeit unevenly. This module equips you to think critically about their transformative potential without losing sight of the present realities of teachers, learners, and communities who are the ultimate beneficiaries or victims of technology decisions. You will synthesise your course learning into an original, innovative, and scalable mathematics EdTech solution design, and critically assess what genuine transformation in mathematics education requires beyond technology.

  1. Identify and describe emerging technologies (AI, AR, VR, and adjacent developments) and their applications in mathematics education.

  2. Analyse the ethical considerations, equity implications, and sustainability challenges of emerging EdTech.

  3. Design an innovative, scalable, and ethically grounded mathematics EdTech solution drawing on course-wide learning.

  4. Critically assess the transformative potential and limitations of emerging technologies in mathematics education.

2 EMERGING TECHNOLOGIES IN MATHEMATICS EDUCATION

2.1 1.1 The Hype Cycle and Mathematics EdTech

Before examining specific technologies, it is essential to apply a critical lens to how educational technology is typically discussed. The Gartner Hype Cycle describes how emerging technologies pass through a predictable pattern of over-enthusiasm followed by disillusionment before settling into productive use:

  1. Technology Trigger: A new technology is announced; early use cases generate media attention.

  2. Peak of Inflated Expectations: Enthusiasts claim the technology will transform everything; early pilots are overpromised and underdelivered.

  3. Trough of Disillusionment: Real-world pilots reveal limitations; media attention turns negative; funding retreats.

  4. Slope of Enlightenment: More nuanced, contextually-aware applications emerge; second-generation tools are better designed.

  5. Plateau of Productivity: Mature, well-understood applications are widely adopted; technology delivers consistent, measurable value.

Every emerging technology in education should be evaluated against the same fundamental question from Module 7: Does this technology address a specific, documented gap in mathematics learning that cannot be adequately closed by simpler, more established means? A technology at the Peak of Inflated Expectations demands the most rigorous application of this question. Enthusiasm is not evidence.

2.2 1.2 Artificial Intelligence in Mathematics Education

Artificial Intelligence (AI) is the umbrella term for computational systems that perform tasks typically associated with human intelligence: pattern recognition, natural language processing, decision-making, and prediction. In mathematics education, AI manifests in several distinct forms:

Adaptive Learning Systems

Already explored in Modules 1 and 8, adaptive learning systems use machine learning algorithms to personalise mathematical content, difficulty, and pacing. Current state of the art:

  • Knowledge tracing: Bayesian and deep learning models that estimate a learner’s mastery of each mathematical concept based on their interaction history.

  • Item response theory (IRT): Statistical models that assess question difficulty and learner ability simultaneously, enabling more precise difficulty calibration.

  • Prerequisite mapping: AI systems that automatically identify which mathematical concepts a learner needs to master before proceeding, building personalised learning pathways.

AI Tutoring Systems and Conversational AI

Intelligent Tutoring Systems (ITS) such as Carnegie Learning’s MATHia use AI to simulate one-on-one tutoring interactions, providing step-by-step scaffolding and error diagnosis. More recently, Large Language Models (LLMs) like GPT-4 and Claude have introduced conversational AI tutoring:

  • Students can ask questions in natural language about any mathematics topic and receive explanations at an appropriate level.

  • AI tutors can generate worked examples tailored to a specific error pattern a student has exhibited.

  • Teachers can use AI to generate differentiated problem sets, mark schemes, and explanatory notes instantly.

Current capabilities:

  • Explain mathematical concepts in clear natural language at any level.

  • Generate practice problems with worked solutions.

  • Identify and explain common mathematical errors.

  • Provide step-by-step guidance through problem-solving.

  • Translate mathematics content into multiple languages.

Current limitations:

  • Mathematical reasoning errors, particularly in multi-step problems and non-standard formats.

  • Hallucination: generating plausible-sounding but incorrect mathematical claims.

  • No persistent memory of individual learner history across sessions (in most implementations).

  • Significant equity barrier: requires reliable internet and modern devices.

  • Risk of students using AI to complete work without engaging with the mathematics.

AI-Powered Assessment and Automated Feedback

AI is transforming mathematics assessment in several ways:

  • Automated marking of free-response work: Computer vision and NLP systems can read handwritten or typed mathematical working, identify the approach used, and award marks accordingly.

  • Error pattern analysis at class level: AI analytics can identify that 60% of a class made the same algebraic manipulation error, enabling targeted reteaching before the next lesson.

  • Predictive analytics: AI systems can flag learners at risk of underperformance based on early-term interaction patterns, enabling proactive teacher intervention.

2.3 1.3 Augmented Reality (AR) in Mathematics Education

Augmented Reality (AR) overlays digital information onto the physical world, typically via a smartphone, tablet, or AR glasses. Unlike Virtual Reality, AR does not replace the real environment — it enhances it.

Mathematics applications of AR:

  • 3D geometry: Students point a device at a flat diagram and see a three-dimensional solid rotate in space above the paper. GeoGebra AR enables this for a range of geometric solids.

  • Measurement and scale: AR tools allow students to measure real objects in their environment and link them to mathematical concepts (area, volume, perimeter).

  • Data visualisation: Statistical data is represented as physical objects in the real world — a bar chart “standing up” on a desk from a data table.

  • Situated problem-solving: Mathematical problems are embedded in the student’s actual physical environment, increasing contextual relevance.

Current barriers to AR in mathematics EdTech:

  • Most high-quality AR requires modern smartphones or tablets with sufficient processing power — a significant equity barrier.

  • Teacher expertise required to integrate AR activities into lesson sequences is currently high.

  • AR content creation is technically demanding; a shortage of curriculum-aligned AR activities exists.

  • Battery drain and device heat during extended AR sessions limit classroom viability.

2.4 1.4 Virtual Reality (VR) in Mathematics Education

Virtual Reality (VR) immerses the user in a completely computer-generated environment, typically via a head-mounted display (HMD) such as Meta Quest or a budget cardboard viewer.

Mathematics applications of VR:

  • Immersive geometry: Students walk through and interact with three-dimensional geometric environments — exploring the interior of a polyhedron, manipulating a torus, or experiencing non-Euclidean geometry from the inside.

  • Embodied mathematics: VR enables learners to physically experience mathematical concepts through movement and gesture — walking along a number line, embodying a function’s transformation, or “being” a point on a Cartesian plane.

  • Virtual manipulatives: Physical manipulatives (fraction bars, base-10 blocks, geometric solids) are reproduced in VR with the added affordance of infinite supply, unlimited scale, and automatic measurement.

  • Collaborative virtual spaces: Multiple students in different physical locations can work together in a shared VR mathematics environment.

Research evidence and limitations: Research on VR in mathematics education is still early-stage. Promising results have been found for spatial reasoning and 3D geometry. However:

  • Hardware cost (quality HMDs range from $300–$500) makes school-scale deployment financially challenging.

  • VR sickness (nausea from extended use) affects a significant minority of users.

  • Sessions are typically limited to 20–30 minutes, constraining instructional utility.

  • The most powerful mathematics VR environments require significant development investment; off-the-shelf curriculum-aligned mathematics VR content is limited.

3 ETHICAL CONSIDERATIONS IN EMERGING EDTECH

3.1 2.1 The Ethics of AI in Mathematics Education

The deployment of AI in mathematics education raises ethical questions that cannot be reduced to technical design choices. They concern power, justice, accountability, and the nature of mathematics learning itself.

Algorithmic Bias and Equity

AI systems trained on historical data inherit the biases present in that data. In mathematics education, this produces specific risks:

  • Training data bias: If an adaptive system is trained primarily on data from well-resourced, English-medium, urban schools, its difficulty calibration and misconception detection may be poorly tuned for rural, multilingual, or under-resourced learners.

  • Performance gap amplification: Adaptive systems may systematically route lower-performing learners (who are disproportionately from marginalised communities) to lower-level content, entrenching rather than closing performance gaps.

  • Language and cultural bias: NLP systems trained primarily on English may perform significantly worse for learners whose mathematical language is not English, systematically disadvantaging them.

Learner Data, Privacy, and Surveillance

AI systems require data. Mathematics EdTech AI systems collect learner performance data at a granularity unprecedented in human educational history: every keystroke, every hint request, every second of processing time. This data:

  • Is collected from children, who cannot fully consent to its implications.

  • May be used for commercial purposes beyond the stated educational function.

  • Creates detailed profiles of learner strengths, weaknesses, and learning styles that could follow learners throughout their educational careers.

  • Is subject to data breaches, with potentially serious consequences for learner privacy.

  • May be stored in jurisdictions with weaker data protection standards than the learner’s home country.

The Automation of Teaching Judgement

When AI systems make decisions about what a learner needs next, they replace the teacher’s professional judgement. This raises questions about:

  • Accountability: If an AI system makes a consistently poor educational decision for a specific learner, who is responsible — the teacher, the school, or the platform provider?

  • Deskilling: If teachers increasingly defer to AI recommendations, will they lose the diagnostic skills that underpin excellent mathematics teaching?

  • Reductionism: AI systems can only optimise for what they can measure. If mathematics education values creativity, persistence, and collaborative reasoning, but the AI only measures procedural accuracy, the measured will crowd out the unmeasured.

In any educational system, what gets measured gets taught — and what gets taught gets valued. AI-powered analytics that measure only procedural accuracy, response time, and completion rates will, over time, create pressure to teach only what those metrics capture. The values that mathematics educators hold most deeply — mathematical thinking, conjecture, proof, creative problem-solving — are precisely those most resistant to algorithmic measurement. This is not an argument against AI; it is an argument for designing AI systems whose measurement choices reflect the full scope of mathematical learning.

3.2 2.2 The Ethics of AR and VR in Mathematics Education

AR and VR raise distinct ethical concerns:

  • Equity of access: VR hardware is expensive; AR requires modern smartphones. Deploying VR/AR in well-resourced schools while under-resourced schools use print textbooks widens the educational technology gap.

  • Health and safety: Extended VR use raises concerns about eye strain, VR sickness, and the impact of immersive environments on children’s developing sensory systems. Age-appropriate use guidelines are still being established.

  • Data collection in immersive environments: VR systems can collect biometric data (eye tracking, movement, physiological responses) of a particularly intimate nature. The ethical framework for this data collection in educational settings is underdeveloped.

  • Reality displacement: The risk that learners who spend significant time in virtual mathematical environments develop weaker ability to apply mathematical thinking in physical, analogue contexts.

3.3 2.3 A Framework for Ethical Evaluation of Emerging EdTech

Before adopting or designing with any emerging technology, evaluate it against five ethical dimensions:

  1. Equity: Who benefits and who is excluded? Does this technology close or widen existing educational gaps? Is it accessible to learners in under-resourced contexts?

  2. Privacy and Data Rights: What data does the technology collect? Who owns it? Where is it stored? Is informed consent meaningful for child learners? What are the data breach risks?

  3. Accountability: When the technology makes a consequential decision about a learner, who is responsible? Is there a human review mechanism? Can decisions be challenged?

  4. Transparency: Can educators, learners, and parents understand how the technology works? Are algorithmic decisions explainable? Is the training data disclosed?

  5. Mathematical Integrity: Does the technology develop genuine mathematical understanding, thinking, and agency? Or does it produce metric-satisfying behaviour without deep learning?

4 SUSTAINABILITY OF EMERGING EDTECH

4.1 3.1 Financial Sustainability of Emerging Technologies

Emerging technologies are typically expensive at introduction and decrease in cost as they mature. However, the pace of this cost decline varies dramatically:

  • AI: The computational cost of AI inference is declining rapidly; access to AI tutoring via APIs is becoming increasingly affordable. However, training large models remains extremely expensive, concentrating power in a few large technology companies.

  • AR: Smartphone-based AR is already accessible at minimal marginal cost in contexts where modern smartphones are available. Dedicated AR glasses remain expensive ($500–$3,500).

  • VR: Standalone VR headsets have declined from $800 to under $300 in five years but remain out of reach for most schools in the Global South. Cardboard VR viewers ($5–$15) offer a low-fidelity entry point.

4.2 3.2 Environmental Sustainability

The environmental cost of digital technology is often invisible in EdTech discussions but is significant and growing:

  • Device production: Manufacturing smartphones, tablets, and VR headsets involves significant carbon emissions and rare earth mineral extraction.

  • Energy consumption: Training large AI models consumes energy equivalent to the lifetime emissions of multiple cars. AI inference at scale has significant ongoing energy costs.

  • E-waste: Short device refresh cycles generate large quantities of electronic waste, disproportionately processed in developing countries with inadequate safety standards.

  • Data centres: Storing and processing learner data in cloud infrastructure has substantial energy costs.

The most environmentally sustainable EdTech intervention is the one that achieves the greatest educational impact with the fewest new devices, the lowest data transfer requirements, and the longest device lifecycle. Open-source, offline-first, low-bandwidth tools on existing devices have a dramatically lower environmental footprint than cloud-dependent AI platforms requiring hardware upgrades. Environmental sustainability should be a design criterion, not an afterthought.

4.3 3.3 Pedagogical Sustainability: The Human in the Loop

The most important sustainability question for emerging EdTech is not financial or environmental — it is pedagogical. Can teachers meaningfully integrate these technologies into their practice in a way that is sustainable over time?

Emerging technologies are pedagogically sustainable when:

  • Teachers understand not just how to operate the technology but how to integrate it into a coherent lesson design.

  • The technology reduces teacher workload in some dimensions while increasing learning quality, rather than adding to already unsustainable workloads.

  • There is ongoing, embedded professional development support.

  • The technology can evolve with the curriculum without requiring complete re-training.

Emerging technologies are pedagogically unsustainable when:

  • They require teachers to develop entirely new instructional approaches that compete with their core identity and expertise.

  • They produce results that teachers cannot interpret or act on.

  • Rapid version changes make yesterday’s training irrelevant.

  • Teachers are positioned as technicians operating the system rather than professionals making educational decisions.

5 ASSESSING TRANSFORMATIVE POTENTIAL

5.1 4.1 What Does “Transformative” Mean in Mathematics Education?

The word “transformative” is used frequently and loosely in EdTech discourse. Before applying it, we need a working definition. In the context of mathematics education, a truly transformative technology is one that enables mathematics learning outcomes, experiences, or access that were structurally impossible before its introduction — not merely more convenient or marginally more effective.

Level

Description

Mathematics EdTech Example

Improvement

Does the same thing faster, cheaper, or more conveniently.

Digital flashcards replacing paper flashcards for times tables.

Enhancement

Does the same thing better, with measurable improvement in outcomes.

Adaptive platform reducing the time to mastery of algebraic procedures by 20%.

Transformation

Enables qualitatively new learning experiences, access, or outcomes impossible without the technology.

A learner in a remote village with no specialist mathematics teacher receiving personalised, responsive instruction from an AI tutor. A blind student exploring 3D geometry through a haptic VR glove.

5.2 4.2 Critical Assessment of AI’s Transformative Potential

AI has genuine transformative potential in mathematics education in three specific areas:

  1. Access transformation: AI tutoring can provide responsive, expert-level mathematical support to learners in contexts where no specialist teacher is available. For the hundreds of millions of learners globally taught mathematics by non-specialist teachers, this is structurally transformative.

  2. Personalisation at scale: No human teacher can simultaneously deliver personalised instruction to 40 learners. AI adaptive systems can — though the quality of this personalisation depends heavily on the sophistication of the model and the quality of the training data.

  3. Diagnostic depth: AI can detect subtle, persistent error patterns in individual learners that would be invisible to a teacher managing a large class, enabling precision interventions.

AI does not have transformative potential in areas where the quality of mathematical learning depends on things AI cannot provide:

  • The relational trust between a teacher and a learner that makes mathematical risk-taking safe.

  • The unpredictable richness of collaborative mathematical discussion.

  • The modelling of mathematical habits of mind by an expert who genuinely loves and struggles with mathematics.

  • The contextual wisdom to know when a learner needs encouragement, when they need challenge, and when they need rest.

The most powerful vision for AI in mathematics education is not AI replacing teachers but AI augmenting teachers: handling the diagnostic and feedback functions that AI does better than humans at scale (pattern detection, instant response, personalised sequencing), while teachers focus on the relational, creative, and values-laden dimensions of mathematical education that only humans can provide. This requires designing AI tools that give power to teachers rather than replacing their professional judgement.

5.3 4.3 Critical Assessment of AR and VR’s Transformative Potential

AR and VR’s transformative potential is most concentrated in spatial and geometric mathematics — an area where the limitations of two-dimensional representation on paper or screen have always constrained learning:

  • Students have consistently struggled with 3D geometry when learning only from flat diagrams. VR enables genuinely immersive 3D exploration.

  • Spatial reasoning — a predictor of mathematical achievement — can be developed through embodied VR experiences in ways print materials cannot support.

  • AR can place mathematical content in the learner’s actual physical environment, creating authentic contextual connections.

However, for the large majority of secondary mathematics content (algebra, functions, data handling, calculus), AR and VR offer enhancement at best, not transformation. A well-designed GeoGebra activity on a 2D screen achieves most of the conceptual visualisation goals of a VR experience for these topics, at a fraction of the cost and complexity.

5.4 4.4 The Equity Test for Transformative Technology

No technology is genuinely transformative if its benefits are available only to learners in well-resourced settings. The equity test asks: Does this technology’s transformative potential reach learners who need transformation most?

Applied to current emerging technologies:

  • AI tutoring: Potentially transformative for learners in under-resourced settings — but only if it is accessible offline, in local languages, and without requiring expensive devices. Current implementations mostly fail this test.

  • AR: Limited transformative potential for equity; smartphone requirements exclude the most under-resourced learners.

  • VR: Fails the equity test decisively at current hardware prices and connectivity requirements.

  • Open-source adaptive tools (Kolibri, Khan Academy): Already reaching learners in low-resource settings, with genuine, measurable impact — arguably more transformative in equity terms than AR/VR despite being less technologically spectacular.

6 DESIGNING AN INNOVATIVE AND SCALABLE EDTECH SOLUTION

6.1 5.1 The Capstone Design Challenge

The culminating activity of this module — and this course — is the design of an innovative, scalable, and ethically grounded mathematics EdTech solution. This solution is not a prototype build (that was Module 8) but a design argument: a coherent, evidence-based case for a solution that integrates course-wide learning from all ten modules.

6.2 5.2 The Design Synthesis Framework

The following framework ensures the solution integrates all course dimensions:

1. Problem Statement (Module 7 — Needs Assessment)
What specific, documented gap in mathematics learning does this solution address? For whom? Where?
2. Context Profile (Module 6 — Options by Context)
What is the resource context (devices, connectivity, power, teacher capacity, language, curriculum)? What are the binding constraints?
3. Technology Selection and Justification (Modules 1, 8, 10)
Which technology or combination of technologies is proposed? What is the evidence base? How does it address the identified gap in this specific context?
4. Inclusive Design (Module 8)
How are UDL principles applied? What gamification or adaptive elements are included? How are diverse learners served?
5. Ethical Analysis (Module 10)
Apply the five-dimension ethical evaluation framework (equity, privacy, accountability, transparency, mathematical integrity).
6. Open-Source and Community Dimension (Module 3)
Is the solution open-source? How will it be contributed to or built from open resources? What is the open licensing strategy?
7. Implementation and Scale Plan (Module 9)
What is the four-phase implementation roadmap? Who are the stakeholders? What are the six scalability factors?
8. Risk Register (Module 9)
What are the top five risks and their mitigation strategies?
9. Sustainability Plan (Modules 7, 9)
How will the solution be sustained beyond initial funding? What is the per-learner cost at scale?
10. Transformative Potential Assessment (Module 10)
Is this solution Improvement, Enhancement, or Transformation? Who benefits from the transformation? Does it pass the equity test?

6.3 5.3 Worked Example: Capstone Solution Design

Solution Title: Hisabati AI — An offline-capable AI mathematics tutor for low-resource East African secondary schools.

Problem Statement: 73% of secondary schools in rural East Africa are taught mathematics by non-specialist teachers (teachers without a mathematics degree or certification). This produces a persistent, documented quality gap in mathematical instruction — particularly in algebra, functions, and statistics. An AI tutor capable of responding to individual student questions, diagnosing errors, and providing worked examples in Swahili could partially substitute for the specialist teacher expertise that is absent.
Context Profile: Targeting Tanzania and Kenya rural secondary schools. Solar power available; 2–3G mobile data intermittently; 1:4 device ratio (shared Android tablets); Swahili medium of instruction; NECTA/KCSE curriculum.
Technology: A locally-hosted, lightweight AI tutoring model (small language model optimised for mathematics, not a full LLM) that runs on a Raspberry Pi server distributed via local WiFi. Students access via any device browser. No cloud dependency; syncs curriculum updates when mobile data is available. Optimised for Swahili mathematics vocabulary.
Inclusive Design: Multiple response formats (text, voice, diagram upload); audio output for low-literacy users; adaptive difficulty; WCAG 2.1 AA compliant; culturally relevant contexts (agricultural pricing, transport distance, mobile phone data budgeting).
Ethical Analysis:

  • Equity: Designed specifically for low-resource context; passes equity test.

  • Privacy: All data stored locally; no cloud upload of learner data; POPIA-compatible.

  • Accountability: Teacher dashboard shows AI recommendations; teacher retains override authority.

  • Transparency: Explainable AI; feedback messages show reasoning.

  • Mathematical integrity: Tutor prioritises conceptual explanation; does not give answers without working.

Open Source: Built on open-source LLM infrastructure (e.g., LLaMA-derived model); content and curriculum mapping under CC BY-SA; hosted on GitHub; community contribution invited for additional language packs and curriculum alignments.
Transformative Potential: Genuine transformation — provides responsive specialist mathematics instruction to learners who have no specialist teacher. Passes equity test. Targets the learners who need transformation most.

7 GROUNDING THE FUTURE IN THE PRESENT: WHAT WE KNOW FOR CERTAIN

7.1 6.1 What Will Not Change

Amid the uncertainty of emerging technology trajectories, several things are highly unlikely to change:

  • The importance of mathematical understanding: Procedural fluency without conceptual understanding remains inadequate preparation for life, work, and further study — regardless of whether AI can perform procedures.

  • The centrality of the teacher: Research evidence across decades and geographies consistently shows that teacher quality is the single most powerful in-school determinant of learning outcomes. No technology has changed this finding.

  • The equity challenge: The learners who most need excellent mathematics education are consistently those with the least access to it. Technology has not reliably changed this. Whether emerging AI, AR, and VR close or widen this gap depends on specific design and policy choices — not on the technology itself.

  • The need for critical educators: Teachers who understand what technology can and cannot do, who can evaluate tools against evidence rather than marketing claims, and who design technology use around learning goals rather than around the technology itself will always be more valuable than those who do not.

7.3 6.3 A Closing Reflection: The Educator as the Constant

Across all ten modules, every framework, every tool, every case study has pointed to the same conclusion: technology is a means, not an end. The end — the purpose around which every EdTech decision must be oriented — is learners developing genuine mathematical understanding, capability, and confidence, particularly those learners who have historically been failed by educational systems that were not designed with them in mind.

The educator who finishes this course is not a technology enthusiast or a technology sceptic. They are a technology realist: someone who knows what the evidence says, who can apply principled frameworks to novel situations, who is not seduced by hype and not paralysed by caution, and who keeps the learner — the actual human being sitting in the actual classroom — at the centre of every decision.

That capacity is not a product of any technology. It is a product of education. It is what this course has been building.

Task: Research the current state of one of the following emerging technologies in mathematics education: AI tutoring systems, AR mathematics tools, or VR mathematics environments. Produce a 500-word briefing document that:

  1. Describes the current state of the technology (what exists, what is in development).

  2. Evaluates it against the Hype Cycle model (where is it currently?).

  3. Applies the ethical evaluation framework (five dimensions).

  4. Assesses its transformative potential using the Improvement / Enhancement / Transformation taxonomy.

8.2 7.2 Activity 2: Ethical Case Analysis

Task: Read the following scenario and produce a structured ethical analysis:

The Ministry of Education of a fictional African country is considering using an AI system to automatically mark open-ended mathematics answers in the national Grade 12 examination. The AI has been trained on 10 years of marked examination scripts from urban schools. It would reduce marking costs by 60% and produce results 6 weeks faster than the current human-marking process. The system is provided by a US company; all scripts would be uploaded to US-based servers for processing. The AI’s marking rationale for any individual script is not available to schools or learners.

Your analysis must apply all five dimensions of the ethical evaluation framework, identify the three most significant risks, and propose specific safeguards for each.

  • Pass the equity test.

  • Be supported by a complete implementation roadmap and risk register.

Artificial Intelligence (AI)

Computational systems that perform tasks typically requiring human intelligence, including pattern recognition, natural language processing, and adaptive decision-making.

Large Language Model (LLM)

A category of AI trained on vast text corpora that can generate human-like text, explain concepts, and engage in educational dialogue, including mathematics tutoring.

Intelligent Tutoring System (ITS)

An AI-powered system designed to simulate one-on-one tutoring, providing adaptive instruction, step-by-step scaffolding, and error-specific feedback.

Hallucination (AI)

The tendency of LLMs to generate plausible-sounding but factually incorrect statements, a significant risk in mathematics contexts where precision is essential.

Augmented Reality (AR)

Technology that overlays digital information onto the physical world, typically via a smartphone, tablet, or AR glasses, enhancing rather than replacing the real environment.

Virtual Reality (VR)

Technology that immerses the user in a completely computer-generated environment via a head-mounted display, enabling embodied mathematical experiences not possible in 2D.

Algorithmic Bias

The systematic and unjust discrimination produced by AI systems whose training data or design reflects historical inequities, producing worse outcomes for already-disadvantaged groups.

Knowledge Tracing

AI technique that models a learner’s mastery of each knowledge component based on their interaction history, used in adaptive learning systems to personalise content sequencing.

Transformative Technology

Technology that enables qualitatively new learning outcomes, experiences, or access that were structurally impossible before its introduction — distinct from improvement or enhancement.

Equity Test

The evaluative question: “Does this technology’s benefits reach the learners who need transformation most, or only those already well-served by existing systems?”

Measurement Displacement

The phenomenon in which educational systems increasingly teach only what AI and data systems can measure, crowding out valuable outcomes (creativity, reasoning, mathematical agency) that resist quantification.

Human-AI Partnership

A model of AI deployment in education in which AI handles diagnostic, adaptive, and feedback functions at scale, while teachers focus on relational, creative, and values-laden dimensions of mathematical education.

Environmental Sustainability (EdTech)

The obligation to consider device production, energy consumption, e-waste, and data centre costs in EdTech design decisions, favouring low-bandwidth, long-lifecycle, and offline-capable solutions.

Technology Realism

The professional disposition of an educator who neither uncritically embraces nor reflexively rejects new technology, but evaluates each tool against evidence, ethical principles, and the specific needs of real learners in real contexts.
