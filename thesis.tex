\section*{Module 5 Summary Guide}
\section*{Start Here}
Goal: Now that you have your research design and ethics clearance sorted out, Module 5 helps you test and fine-tune your data collection instruments and procedures before the real data collection. Piloting means doing a small-scale trial of your survey, interview, or other method to catch problems early. By the end of this module, you will have a revised, improved instrument (questionnaire, interview guide, etc.), a log of changes you made based on pilot feedback, and a clear plan for your full data collection rollout.

Before You Begin: Identify a handful of people similar to your target participants who can help test your instrument. For example, if your study is surveying university students, grab a few fellow students to try the survey. If it's interviews with teachers, perhaps find one teacher acquaintance for a practice interview (if possible). Also, review the goals of your instrument what exactly you need to capture with each question or section so you know what to look for in the pilot results (are you getting the kind of data you need?).

\section*{Why This Matters}
Jumping into full data collection without a pilot is like launching a rocket without a test flight - risky and potentially costly. Piloting matters because it reveals issues: confusing survey questions, improper skip logic, interview questions that respondents don't understand or that yield superficial answers, technical problems with an online form, etc. By ironing these out in a pilot, you ensure your actual data is high-quality and that you won't waste time later re-collecting or regretting poorly worded questions. This module ensures your instrument is reliable (consistent) and valid (capturing what it's supposed to). It also helps refine your techniques. For instance, you might discover during a pilot interview that you need to be better at probing for detailed answers. Ultimately, a good pilot makes the real data collection smoother, more effective, and more credible.

\section*{What you will Do}
During Module 5, you will:

\begin{itemize}
  \item Decide what to pilot: you will determine which aspects of your study to test. It could be the entire survey, a subset of survey questions (especially new or critical ones), the interview guide questions, the procedure of approaching participants, the data recording process, etc. (Often, all instruments get a pilot on a small scale.)
  \item Conduct a pilot study: This could take various forms:
  \item Cognitive Interviews: where you ask a few people to think aloud as they answer your survey questions to see if they interpret them as intended.
  \item Small-N Field Pilot: e.g., collect a few survey responses (maybe 10-20), or do 1-2 practice interviews or focus groups.
  \item Usability test: if using an online survey or tool, have someone use it while observing them for any difficulties (on different devices if applicable).
  \item Possibly an expert review: get a mentor or peer to review your instrument content for clarity and relevance.
  \item Evaluate pilot results: you will gather the feedback and data from the pilot and analyze it for issues:
  \item Identify any confusing questions or instructions (e.g., participants asked "What do you mean by this?" or they selected "Other" a lot because your categories missed something).
  \item Look at responses for content validity: are responses aligning with what you sought? (e.g., if a question was supposed to differentiate people but everyone gave the same answer, maybe it's not discriminating well).
  \item If quantitative, do a quick check of any multi-item scale reliability (with pilot data, maybe use Cronbach's alpha if there are enough responses).
  \item Time yourself or participants to see if the length is reasonable (maybe your survey takes 30 minutes when you expected 10 - that's a problem to fix).
  \item Revise the instrument: Based on the pilot insights, make changes. This could mean rewording or removing questions, adding answer options, adjusting the order of questions, improving interview prompts, fixing technical issues, etc. Document these changes.
  \item Update supporting materials: If you change content that affects consent or info sheets (like adding a sensitive question), update those if needed. Also plan any re-training or briefing if you have others helping in data collection.
  \item Create a change log: A simple table or list capturing what you changed after the pilot and why (the evidence from pilot). This transparency is useful for your methodology chapter to show you improved your instrument, and if applicable, you might need to submit a summary of pilot findings or a change log for assessment.
  \item Finalize the deployment-ready instrument: Ensure the version is marked (like "Questionnaire v1.1 - revised after pilot") and locked in for use. Also finalize things like versioning (date on forms, etc.) now so you're consistent during data collection.
\end{itemize}

\section*{What to Pilot (Key Focus Areas)}
When piloting, pay attention to these common instrument issues:

\begin{itemize}
  \item Question wording: Are any questions unclear or ambiguous? Long, complex sentences can confuse people. Look for leading or loaded phrasing that\\
might bias answers. Watch for double-barreled questions (asking two things at once like "How satisfied are you with your salary and job conditions?" split those!). Ensure language is appropriate for your participants' reading level and background.
  \item Response options: Check that your multiple-choice or scale responses cover all common answers. Did participants frequently choose "Other" and write something you hadn't listed? If so, add that option. Are scale endpoints and labels clear (e.g., what does 1 vs 5 mean)? Ensure ranges make sense (for numerical answers, provide units or context).
  \item Flow \& length: Did participants show fatigue or rush through later parts? Maybe the instrument is too long or needs a more engaging flow. Check ordering - sometimes one question can influence answers to the next (order effects), so consider if you need randomization or reordering.
  \item Skip logic and technicals: In an online survey, make sure skip logic (if any) worked correctly. Any error messages or confusion about required fields? On paper, did anyone accidentally skip a section? Maybe you need clearer section headings or instructions like "If you answered Yes to Q5, go to Q6; if No, skip to Q10."
  \item Administration mode issues: If phone or face-to-face, could the interviewer capture responses easily? If online, was the interface okay on mobile phones as well as computers? If any accessibility needs (for differently-abled participants), did you account for those (like screen reader compatibility, etc.)?
  \item Time burden: If you promised a 10 -minute survey but the pilot shows it takes 25 , you must cut or simplify items to reduce fatigue and be honest in your info to participants.
  \item Sensitive questions comfort: If you have personal or sensitive questions, gauge reactions. If people hesitated or felt uneasy, consider rephrasing to be more sensitive or adding a gentle preface. In interviews, maybe move sensitive questions later once rapport is built.
  \item Data quality checks: For quant, glance if any respondents gave the same answer for all items in a section (could indicate boredom or misunderstanding of scale). For qual, did your questions elicit detailed answers or just yes/no? If the latter, you need to ask more open-ended or follow-up questions.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Rapid Pilot Designs (Options)}
Depending on your study, you might employ one or more of these:

\begin{itemize}
  \item Cognitive Interviewing (5-10 participants): Especially for surveys, ask participants to explain their thought process for each question ("What does\\
this question mean to you? How did you decide on that answer?"). This uncovers misinterpretations.
  \item Small-N Field Pilot: If doing a survey, get about $20-50$ responses to do some preliminary analysis (even simple frequency counts can show odd patterns). If doing interviews/focus groups, do a couple and see if your questions yield the type of discussion you want, and test your recording equipment and note-taking approach.
  \item Usability Test (if using tech): Watch someone navigate your online survey or app. Note any difficulties like having to scroll horizontally, unclear buttons, etc. Test on different devices/browsers.
  \item Expert Review: Have $2-3$ experts (maybe your supervisor, or peers experienced in research) review your instruments and give feedback on content coverage and alignment with objectives. They might catch things you miss.
  \item Focus on challenging parts: If time is limited, you might pilot only the hardest section - e.g., a new scale you developed or a sensitive interview question - rather than everything. Though a full pilot is ideal, a targeted one is better than none.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Quick Quality Checks Post-Pilot}
After the pilot, perform these quick evaluations:

\begin{itemize}
  \item Content validity check: Do items cover the construct fully? (Ask: are any important sub-aspects of a concept not touched by any question? If yes, add an item. Or did the pilot show some questions that were redundant? If yes, maybe cut one).
  \item Response process evidence: From cognitive interviews or feedback, did participants interpret questions consistently and as intended? Note any systematic misunderstandings and fix wording.
  \item Reliability (for surveys with scales): If applicable, compute Cronbach's alpha for multi-item scales using pilot data. If reliability is low, investigate if some items don't correlate with the rest - they might be poorly worded or unrelated and could be dropped or revised.
  \item Interview guide coherence: If qualitative, did your interview flow well? Did you get shallow answers? Maybe add probes. Did you inadvertently lead the participant with a phrasing? Adjust that. Ensure you have prompts for deeper inquiry where needed.
  \item Timing and engagement: Check how long each part took and if engagement dropped. Possibly shuffle interesting questions earlier or break up monotony with different question types.
  \item Data recording quality: If recorded audio, is the quality clear? If notes, did you capture enough detail? Adjust strategies (e.g., maybe use a better microphone or find quieter interview locations).
\end{itemize}

\section*{Refinement Workflow}
Use a systematic approach to refine:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item Log issues: Create a table listing each problem encountered by item/section (e.g., "Q3 confusing wording; participant thought 'income' meant gross, not net"). Also log any observed problem like "interviewees hesitant on Q5."
  \item Decide action: For each issue, decide to keep as is, revise, drop, or add something. Write a rationale: e.g., "Revise Q3 wording to clarify 'monthly take-home income' to avoid confusion."
  \item Implement changes: Reword or reformat accordingly. Use plain language and specificity. For surveys, avoid vague terms like "often" without a timeframe; make it "often (at least once a week)" etc. For scales, ensure consistent anchors.
  \item Re-pilot if needed: If you had major changes, and time permits, test the risky changes again with a couple of people. If time doesn't, at least do an expert or colleague review on the revised version.
  \item Lock version and document: Finalize the instrument (mark it final version for use, with a date). Also, prepare a short Instrument Technical Note basically documentation of your instrument: its purpose, any scoring (for scales), how it was developed and refined, and any limitations. This is useful for transparency and when writing your methodology chapter.
\end{enumerate}
